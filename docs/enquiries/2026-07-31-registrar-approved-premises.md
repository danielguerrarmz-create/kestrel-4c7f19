# Enquiry: can a permanent garden structure be approved premises?

**To:** the Register Office / Approved Premises officer, Isle of Wight Council
**Why this one first:** Northcourt Manor is on the Isle of Wight, so their authority is the one whose
answer can actually be used in a letter to Northcourt. Approved-premises policy varies by local
authority, which is exactly why this cannot be answered from the regulations alone.

**Do not publish anything from §5 of the venue spec until this comes back.** The argument is strong
enough that getting it wrong would be expensive: it would be quoted to a venue owner who would then
check it with their own registrar.

---

## Where to send it

Isle of Wight Council's register office handles approved premises applications; the ceremonies or
registration team is the right desk, not planning. Look for the "approved premises" contact on
iow.gov.uk rather than the general enquiries address — a general inbox will route it to licensing,
which handles a different licence entirely and will answer the wrong question.

## The email

> **Subject:** Approved premises: eligibility of a permanent open-sided garden structure
>
> Dear Sir or Madam,
>
> I am a designer working with country houses on the Island that hold civil ceremonies, and I would
> be grateful for guidance on approved premises before we take a design further.
>
> We design permanent garden pavilions: timber structures on screw-pile foundations, built on site
> and intended to stand for decades. They are open-sided, roofed, and planted so that climbing plants
> grow over the frame. They are not marquees and are not demountable in the way a temporary structure
> is.
>
> Three questions.
>
> **1.** Can a structure of that kind be approved in its own right, as a permanently immovable
> structure comprising at least a room, given that it is open-sided rather than enclosed? I
> understand that anything in the open air, a tent, a marquee or other temporary structure is not
> eligible, and I would like to know where an open-sided permanent building sits against that test.
> If enclosure is required, I would be grateful to know what standard it is judged against, since
> that is a design decision we would make differently from the outset.
>
> **2.** Where a house already holds an approval for a room indoors, would such a structure be
> treated as a linked outdoor area under that existing approval, or would it require a separate
> application?
>
> **3.** Does the Council apply a seasonal restriction to ceremonies held in linked outdoor areas —
> for instance limiting them to British Summer Time — and does that restriction apply differently to
> a permanent roofed structure than to an open lawn? I have seen a summer-only restriction referred
> to elsewhere and would like to know the Island's position. I would also be grateful to know whether
> the restriction on food and drink during proceedings applies in the same way.
>
> I appreciate these are questions of policy as much as regulation, and I am not asking for a
> decision on a specific site. General guidance would be very helpful, and if a pre-application
> conversation is the better route I would be glad to arrange one.
>
> With thanks,
>
> Clay Seifert
> Bower
> clay@bowerbuild.org

## Why each question is asked the way it is

- **Q1 is the whole thing, and the phrasing is doing work.** The regulations say approved premises
  must be "a permanently immovable structure comprising at least a room". A Bower is permanently
  immovable. Whether an open-sided pavilion is "a room" is the entire question and it is genuinely
  arguable, so it is asked directly rather than assumed. **The follow-up about the enclosure standard
  is the commercially valuable part**: if the answer is "it must be enclosed on three sides", that is
  a design input, and knowing it now costs nothing while learning it after a commission costs a
  redesign.
- **Q2 separates the two routes.** A house with an indoor licence may be able to treat the structure
  as a linked outdoor area, which is a much lighter path than a fresh approval. The two have
  different costs and timescales, and a venue owner will want to know which conversation they are
  having.
- **Q3 is where the shoulder-season argument lives or dies.** If the Island restricts linked outdoor
  areas to British Summer Time, then approval as a linked area gets you nothing in January and the
  case has to rest on Q1 — approval in its own right. That distinction is the difference between
  "a room you can marry in when the lawn is unusable" and a summer-only amenity.

## What the answers unlock

| Answer | What goes on the site |
|---|---|
| Yes to Q1, no seasonal limit | The `ceremony-registrar` section, at the **top** of `/houses`. The strongest argument available: a marquee can never be approved premises, so this is the shoulder-season case made in law rather than in adjectives. |
| Yes to Q1, summer-only under Q3 | Still publishable, but as approval **in its own right** rather than as a linked area, and the January claim must be checked against that route specifically. |
| No to Q1 | Publish nothing. Record the enclosure standard in `pending.ts` as a design input and revisit if the product changes. |

Whatever comes back, it is a fact about **one** authority. Anything published has to be worded so it
does not promise the same answer in Wiltshire — "on the Isle of Wight the registrar has confirmed"
is publishable; "civil ceremonies can be held in a Bower" is not.
